# USP 797 vs NAPRA: which clean room rules apply in Canada?

*By Dal Riarh, President and CEO, The Medico Group. Last updated 27 November 2026.*

In Canada, pharmacy clean rooms are built to the NAPRA model standards as adopted by each provincial regulator, not to USP 797 or USP 800. USP chapters are US standards, but much of the cleanroom content, vendor literature and pricing you will find online is written for them. This guide maps the US terms to the Canadian ones and flags the differences that change what gets built.

## Which standard applies to a Canadian pharmacy clean room?

**The NAPRA model standards, once your provincial regulator adopts them. In BC, PODSA bylaw s. 23.7 makes the two NAPRA sterile standards mandatory.**

NAPRA is the national association of pharmacy regulators. It writes model standards, and each provincial college decides whether and when to adopt them. In BC, owners and managers must comply with the NAPRA non-hazardous and hazardous sterile standards "as amended from time to time" ([CPBC PODSA bylaws](https://library.bcpharmacists.org/6_Resources/6-1_Provincial_Legislation/5082-PODSA_Bylaws.pdf)). They came into effect as College standards on 4 July 2022 ([CPBC](https://www.bcpharmacists.org/node/2694)). In Alberta, the college treats the NAPRA model standards as "the minimum requirements" for sterile compounding and inspects pharmacies that compound against them ([ACP](https://abpharmacy.ca/news/does-your-pharmacy-provide-sterile-compounding/)).

USP still appears in Canadian documents. NAPRA's non-sterile standard defines Level A as "simple and moderate compounds, as defined in USP General chapter <795>" ([NAPRA non-sterile](https://www.napra.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/NAPRA-Mdl-Stnds-Pharmacy-Compounding-Nonsterile-Preparations-EN-March-2018-CLAR-Jan-2022.pdf)). A 2009 Canadian hospital survey also discussed renovations to meet USP, noting they "may cost an organization more than US$1 million" ([CJHP](https://cjhp-online.ca/index.php/cjhp/article/download/788/931/3240)). But the numbers a BC inspector checks come from NAPRA and the CPBC review form.

## Why do so many clean room suppliers talk about USP 797?

**Most commercial cleanroom content comes from US vendors and from Canadian modular suppliers selling into the US, so it follows USP.**

When we reviewed the pages that show up for pharmacy cleanroom searches in Canada, the commercial results were mostly modular suppliers and US vendors. Their pages are written for USP 797 and 800 and mention NAPRA only in passing, if at all. None explained provincial rules such as CPBC layout approval. That is not wrong for a US buyer, but a Canadian owner reading a USP spec sheet needs to translate it.

## How do USP terms map to NAPRA terms?

**Most terms map directly: a USP buffer room is a NAPRA clean room, and a C-SEC is the negative-pressure hazardous clean room.**

| USP 797 / 800 term | NAPRA / CPBC term | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cleanroom (one word) | Clean room | Both used; NAPRA uses two words |
| Buffer room | Clean room | The ISO 7 room where compounding happens |
| Ante area / anteroom | Anteroom | NAPRA expects a full anteroom, not an open ante area |
| Primary engineering control (PEC) | PEC / C-PEC | LAFW or CAI for non-hazardous; Class II or III BSC or CACI for hazardous |
| Secondary engineering control (SEC) | Clean room and anteroom suite | |
| Containment SEC (C-SEC) | Hazardous clean room under negative pressure | ISO 7, -2.5 Pa under NAPRA |
| Segregated compounding area (SCA / C-SCA) | No direct equivalent | NAPRA requires anteroom plus clean room suites |
| CSP (compounded sterile preparation) | Sterile preparation | |
| 503A / 503B | Not used | US pharmacy and outsourcing categories |
| Category 1 / 2 / 3 | Not used | NAPRA uses non-hazardous and hazardous sterile, and Levels A to C for non-sterile |

Sources: [Setra on anteroom vs ante area](https://www.setra.com/blog/difference-between-anteroom-and-antearea); [NAPRA hazardous sterile standard](https://www.napra.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/NAPRA-Mdl-Stnds-Pharmacy-Compounding-Hazardous-Sterile-Preparations-Sep-2016-Revised-b.pdf); [CPBC pre-opening checklist](https://library.bcpharmacists.org/7_Forms/7-3_Pharmacy/9120-HOS_PY_Preopening_Inspection_Report_Photo_Checklist.pdf).

## Where do the differences change what gets built?

**Three places: NAPRA has no segregated compounding area, it ties certification to CETA guides, and hazardous rooms need full anteroom suites with outside exhaust.**

**No segregated compounding area.** US rules allow a segregated compounding area for some preparations with short beyond-use dates, outside a full clean room suite ([Minnesota Board of Pharmacy](https://mn.gov/boards/assets/SterileCompoundingAreaRemodelGuidancePharmacy%2010.28.2021_tcm21-506511.pdf)). We found no NAPRA equivalent: the text requires full anteroom plus clean room suites. A CACI may sit in a less clean room only if every listed condition in s. 5.3.3.1 is met. A US design that relies on an SCA will not meet a Canadian inspection as drawn.

**Certification to CETA.** NAPRA requires C-PECs to be certified every 6 months to CETA CAG-003, CAG-005 and CAG-002-2006 (s. 5.3.3.1). US certifiers have published Q&As on why the current USP 797 removed references to CETA ([Pace](https://www.pacelabs.com/building-sciences/usp-797-qa-cleanroom-requirements/)). A Canadian certification plan should name the CETA guides.

**Hazardous suites.** NAPRA's hazardous clean room is negative at -2.5 Pa, the anteroom is ISO 7 at least +5.0 Pa to the pharmacy, both get at least 30 ACPH, and clean room return air is exhausted outside, low on the walls. A shared hazardous and non-hazardous anteroom is allowed but "not recommended", and two clean rooms are required.

**Temporary rooms during renovation.** The same US guidance lists a temporary cleanroom, a satellite, a segregated area, outsourcing or a mobile unit as ways to keep compounding during a remodel. In our reading of NAPRA, a temporary room in BC is itself a sterile compounding area that has to be certified, and probably reported to the College, before use. Budget it as real scope. Our [hospital pharmacy phasing](/pharmacy-clean-rooms/guides/hospital-pharmacy-phasing/) guide covers it.

## What about CSA standards?

**Health authority projects also call up CSA Z8000 and CSA Z317.2. Neither replaces NAPRA; in practice designers apply both and use the stricter value.**

Fraser Health's design requirements say facilities must be designed to CSA Z8000 ([Fraser Health TDR](https://www.fraserhealth.ca/-/media/Project/FraserHealth/FraserHealth/Technical-Design-Requirements/Combined/Technical_Design_Requirements_Combined_Sections.pdf)), and Alberta Health Services refers to CSA Z317.2 Table 1 for HVAC by room type ([AHS](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/healthinfo/ipc/hi-ipc-hcf-design.pdf)). The CSA standards are paywalled, and we do not quote clause values here. Our [CSA Z8000 guide](/csa-z8000-standard/) explains the standard.

## Is US cost data useful for a Canadian project?

**As direction only. US sources put USP 797 compounding cleanrooms at about US$500 to $1,200 per sq ft, with HVAC 35 to 55 percent.**

Those figures come from a US cost publication ([Terrapin](https://terrapincg.com/news/cleanroom-construction-cost-per-square-foot-2026)). Canadian public tenders give better reference points, from $0.35M for a small hospital NAPRA upgrade to $6.25M for Peace Arch Hospital's sterile compounding suites ([Peace Arch listing](https://projects.constructconnect.com/details/7522252-rfp-construction-management-services-for-the-peace-arch-hospital-sterile-compound&find_loc=-V4B-2R4)). Our [pharmacy clean room cost](/pharmacy-clean-rooms/guides/pharmacy-clean-room-cost/) guide covers both.

## Will the new NAPRA documents close the gap with USP?

**Unknown. NAPRA's Board accepted new non-sterile and sterile model documents in 2026, but they are not published or in effect yet.**

NAPRA says that even once published, the documents are not in effect until each regulator adopts them ([NAPRA update](https://www.napra.ca/update-on-napras-model-documents-for-pharmacy-regulatory-authority-use-on-non-sterile-and-sterile-compounding-standards/)). Until then, design to the NAPRA standard your college has adopted. See [NAPRA cleanroom requirements](/pharmacy-clean-rooms/guides/napra-cleanroom-requirements/).

## FAQ

### Does USP 797 apply in Canada?
Not as a legal requirement. Canadian pharmacies follow the NAPRA model standards as adopted by their provincial regulator. USP is referenced in places, such as NAPRA's definition of Level A non-sterile compounding.

### Is a buffer room the same as a clean room?
Yes. USP's buffer room is the ISO 7 room where compounding happens; NAPRA and CPBC call it the clean room.

### Can I use a US cleanroom design in BC?
Only after checking it against NAPRA. Look for segregated compounding areas, open ante areas and certification to non-CETA methods, which may not pass a BC inspection.

### What is the NAPRA equivalent of USP 800?
NAPRA's Model Standards for Pharmacy Compounding of Hazardous Sterile Preparations, plus Level C of the non-sterile standard. WorkSafeBC rules on hazardous drugs also apply in BC.

### Does NAPRA allow a segregated compounding area?
We found no equivalent in the NAPRA text. It requires anteroom plus clean room suites, with narrow conditions for CACIs.
