# Hazardous drug compounding rooms

*By Dal Riarh, President and CEO, The Medico Group. Last updated 27 November 2026.*

A hazardous drug compounding room is a negative-pressure ISO Class 7 clean room, entered through a positive ISO 7 anteroom, with at least 30 air changes an hour and its return air exhausted outside. It protects staff and the rest of the pharmacy from the drugs being prepared. We build these rooms for hospital oncology and IV pharmacies and for private compounding labs, sterile and non-sterile.

## What does a hazardous sterile compounding room need?

**A clean room at -2.5 Pa, an ISO 7 anteroom at +5.0 Pa or more, 30 ACPH in both, and low outside exhaust.**

These values come from NAPRA's model standard for hazardous sterile preparations, which BC makes mandatory through PODSA bylaw s. 23.7 ([NAPRA](https://www.napra.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/NAPRA-Mdl-Stnds-Pharmacy-Compounding-Hazardous-Sterile-Preparations-Sep-2016-Revised-b.pdf); [CPBC bylaws](https://library.bcpharmacists.org/6_Resources/6-1_Provincial_Legislation/5082-PODSA_Bylaws.pdf)).

**NAPRA hazardous sterile requirements that change the build**

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Clean room pressure | -2.5 Pa (0.01 in. water column), at least 2.5 Pa differential to the anteroom |
| Anteroom | ISO Class 7, at least +5.0 Pa relative to the pharmacy |
| Air changes | At least 30 ACPH in the clean room and the anteroom |
| Exhaust | Clean room return air exhausted to the exterior, returns low on the walls |
| C-PEC | Class II or III biological safety cabinet (BSC) or compounding aseptic containment isolator (CACI), ISO 5, running 24 hours a day |
| HD storage room | -2.5 Pa, at least 12 ACPH, all air exhausted outside |
| Water | No sinks or drains in the clean room; allowed in the anteroom |
| Fridge | Biomedical grade; if in the clean room, an exhaust behind it |
| Monitoring | Pressure instruments calibrated at least once a year |
| Certification | C-PEC every 6 months to CETA CAG-003, CAG-005 and CAG-002-2006; particle counts at least every 6 months |

Source: NAPRA hazardous sterile standard, s. 5.3, Tables 2 and 3. CPBC also requires continuous pressure measurement with an alarm and controlled rooms at 20°C or less ([CPBC review form](https://library.bcpharmacists.org/5_Programs/5-2_PRP/5287-PRP_Sterile_Compounding_Review.pdf)).

The exhaust is what drives the building work. A ducted route to the outside, a roof or wall penetration, a fan sized for the room and a make-up air path all have to fit the building before the room layout is final.

## Can hazardous and non-hazardous rooms share an anteroom?

**NAPRA allows it but does not recommend it. A shared anteroom must exhaust all its air outside, and the site still needs two clean rooms.**

The standard says this layout "is not recommended" and that "all air flowing within the shared anteroom must be exhausted to the exterior" (s. 5.3.2.6). Hazardous and non-hazardous work "must have two clean rooms". One way to keep a shared entry without a shared anteroom is a common gowning room that feeds two separate anterooms. That is the layout in the modular pod at our [Coastal Care Compounding Lab](/pharmacy-clean-rooms/projects/coastal-care-compounding-lab/), where a gowning room leads to a non-hazardous anteroom and clean room on one side and a hazardous anteroom and clean room on the other.

## Where do hazardous drugs get stored?

**In a dedicated negative-pressure storage room with 12 ACPH and full outside exhaust, or with their dedicated fridge inside the hazardous clean room.**

NAPRA's Table 3 sets the storage room at -2.5 Pa and at least 12 ACPH, with all air exhausted to the exterior. The alternative is to keep hazardous preparations and their dedicated fridge in the clean room. Damaged hazardous shipments also need a C-PEC for unpacking (s. 5.3.2.5), so receiving needs a plan. The CPBC hospital pre-opening checklist asks for HD storage as a "dedicated room, and/or" in the clean room ([CPBC checklist](https://library.bcpharmacists.org/7_Forms/7-3_Pharmacy/9120-HOS_PY_Preopening_Inspection_Report_Photo_Checklist.pdf)).

## Can an existing non-hazardous room be converted to hazardous?

**Often, yes, but the conversion is mostly mechanical: reversing pressure, adding outside exhaust and raising the anteroom to ISO 7 with 30 ACPH.**

Health authorities tender these conversions on their own. Richmond Hospital's pharmacy HVAC upgrade, listed at $970,800, reclassified the pharmacy lab from non-hazardous to hazardous sterile compounding, with supply, return and exhaust upgrades to the chemo prep room, anteroom and IV room ([Richmond listing](https://projects.constructconnect.com/details/6637858-richmond-hospital-pharmacy-hvac-upgrade&find_loc=-V6X-1A1)). On a hospital job like that, the pharmacy usually keeps compounding during the work, so the phasing plan matters as much as the ductwork. Our [hospital pharmacy phasing](/pharmacy-clean-rooms/guides/hospital-pharmacy-phasing/) guide covers the options.

## What do WorkSafeBC and the health authorities add?

**WorkSafeBC requires a ventilated enclosure and an exposure control plan for hazardous drugs. Health authorities add CSA standards and infection control during construction.**

Since 1 December 2023, WorkSafeBC's OHS Regulation Part 6 uses the term "hazardous drug", requires "a ventilated enclosure" for mixing, preparing and priming specific hazardous drugs, and requires exposure control plans to be developed, reviewed and updated ([WorkSafeBC](https://worksafebc.com/about-us/news-events/announcements/2023/November/new-hazardous-drug-requirements-come-into-effect-december-1)). That applies to every employer, hospital or private.

On health authority projects, contracts call up CSA Z8000 and CSA Z317.13 for construction infection control, and Interior Health's design guidance also points to the BC Health Authorities' Exposure Control Plan for Hazardous Drugs ([Interior Health](https://www.interiorhealth.ca/sites/default/files/PDFS/staff-safety-guidelines-for-healthcare-facility-design-projects.pdf)). In Alberta, AHS design requirements list sterile compounding pharmacies as a restricted area and refer to CSA Z317.2 Table 1 for HVAC by room type ([AHS](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/healthinfo/ipc/hi-ipc-hcf-design.pdf)). Our [CSA Z8000 guide](/csa-z8000-standard/) explains the standard.

## What about hazardous non-sterile compounding?

**A NAPRA Level C non-sterile room is a separate room under negative pressure, externally vented through HEPA, with a C-PEC and an eyewash.**

Non-sterile hazardous work does not need an ISO clean room, but it does need containment. NAPRA's Level C room must be physically separated, negative to surrounding rooms, fitted with a C-PEC and an eyewash, and certified every 6 months ([NAPRA non-sterile](https://www.napra.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/NAPRA-Mdl-Stnds-Pharmacy-Compounding-Nonsterile-Preparations-EN-March-2018-CLAR-Jan-2022.pdf)). ## FAQ

### What pressure does a hazardous drug clean room need?
-2.5 Pa, with at least a 2.5 Pa differential to its anteroom. The anteroom is held at least +5.0 Pa relative to the pharmacy, per NAPRA.

### How many air changes does a chemo compounding room need?
At least 30 ACPH in both the clean room and the anteroom under NAPRA's hazardous sterile standard. A dedicated HD storage room needs at least 12 ACPH.

### Do I need a BSC or a CACI?
NAPRA allows a Class II or Class III BSC or a CACI as the C-PEC. It must run 24 hours a day and be certified every 6 months.

### Does the HD storage room need to be negative pressure?
Yes, -2.5 Pa with at least 12 ACPH and all air exhausted outside, unless the preparations and their dedicated fridge are kept in the hazardous clean room.

### Can the anteroom have a sink?
Yes. NAPRA does not allow sinks or drains in the clean room, but permits them in the anteroom.
